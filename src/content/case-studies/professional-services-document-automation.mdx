## The Problem

A consulting firm of fifteen people was generating the same kinds of documents on repeat: proposals, engagement letters, and client status reports. Every one was started from scratch or patched together from old files, then rewritten to fit the client. Drafting consumed six or more hours per week of senior people's time — the most expensive hours in the firm — and quality varied with whoever happened to write the draft.

The real cost wasn't the hours themselves. It was that the firm's most billable people were doing the work of a template, and clients were waiting on documents that should have been routine.

## The Approach

We began with one process: proposal drafting, the document with the clearest cost and the most direct link to revenue. The success metric was simple — time from "won the opportunity" to "proposal in the client's inbox."

The approach was a template library combined with AI drafting and a mandatory human review step. The firm's own structure, voice, and past successful proposals became the pattern. The AI's job was to produce a strong first draft from a handful of inputs: client name, scope, timeline, and fee structure. The consultant's job was to review, adjust, and own the final document. AI drafts; humans decide.

## The Solution

A document generation workflow now takes a few structured inputs and produces a complete first draft of a proposal, engagement letter, or status report in seconds. Drafts follow the firm's templates, use its language, and include the client's specifics — no blank pages, no repurposed old files.

The review step was built in deliberately. Every document is reviewed by a human before it goes out, which keeps quality control where it belongs while eliminating the hours of staring at a blank page.

## The Results

- Drafting time for recurring documents cut by roughly **70%** — from hours to minutes
- Consistency improved: documents now follow one voice and one structure, every time
- The recovered hours went to client work and business development instead of formatting

The firm didn't replace its writers. It removed the mechanical part of writing — and gave its senior people back the part of the week they're actually paid for.
